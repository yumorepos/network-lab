## 0. Verdict first

**Hire signal: strong on judgment, communication, and data engineering.
Blocked on one domain-fundamental defect that must be fixed before this repo
is shown to anyone.**

This is the top decile of portfolio projects on epistemics. The author leads
with a negative result, flags every demand number observed vs modeled,
documents 13 real limitations with the design decision each one forced, and
maintains an integrity report that records where earlier versions of his own
analysis were wrong and demoted his own recommendations. That is rare and it
is the hardest thing to fake.

It also contains a units error in the core unit-economics calculation that
inflates every load factor and margin in the project by roughly 2x, and that
survived four separate self-review passes. For a network planning role, the
one-way-vs-round-trip convention is the domain's most basic unit discipline.

If I interviewed this candidate, my first question would be "walk me through
how you get from annual O&D passengers to load factor." If he catches it live,
strong hire. If he does not, no hire. **So fix it first, then the honest
disclosure of the fix becomes one of the strongest things in the repo.**

## 1. P0 defect: passenger directionality (2x error)

### The finding

`models/economics.py:187` computes annual departures as:

```python
deps_yr = freq_wk * 52
pax_per_dep = spill.expected_boardings(carried / deps_yr, seats)
```

`freq_wk` is round trips per week. The repo confirms this reading itself:
`INTEGRITY_REPORT.md:122` calls the old hardcoded 7x schedule "daily mainline",
and 3x weekly is only a coherent schedule as round trips. A route flown 7x
weekly therefore operates **728 flights per year, not 364**.

Meanwhile `carried` is bidirectional. All three demand paths produce
both-direction annual passengers, and I verified each:

| path | code | direction |
|---|---|---|
| StatCan anchor | `demand.py:86` filters `Estimates='Number of outbound and inbound passengers'` | both |
| DB1B observed | `demand.py:33` `WHERE origin_cm = hub OR dest_cm = hub`, grouped by the other endpoint | both |
| gravity | `gravity.py:37` groups on `least(origin_cm,dest_cm), greatest(...)` | both |

So the model divides both-direction passengers by one-direction departures.
There is no compensating factor anywhere in the chain: `competition.py`
computes competitor `freq_wk` as directional segment departures, which is
internally consistent for the QSI share ratio and does not offset this.

Cross-check against reality. T-100 truth for Alaska out of SEA in 2024 gives
136 to 158 passengers per departure at load factors of 0.79 to 0.90 on
real aircraft. The model's arithmetic, applied to a market of that size,
produces roughly double the boardings per flight.

### The blast radius

Every `load_factor`, `margin_pct`, `belf`, `annual_contribution`, `rasm_c`,
and therefore every verdict in the repo. I recomputed the full scenario grid
with `deps_yr = freq_wk * 52 * 2`, holding everything else constant:

| study | as shipped | corrected |
|---|---|---|
| Porter YYZ | 5 LAUNCH / 3 MONITOR / 71 PASS | **1 LAUNCH / 1 MONITOR / 77 PASS** |
| WestJet YYC | 0 / 0 / 76 | 0 / 0 / 76 (unchanged) |
| Alaska SEA | 48 PASS / 1 MONITOR | 49 PASS |

Per-market, the five headline Porter LAUNCHes:

| market | shipped verdict | shipped margin | corrected verdict | corrected margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boston | LAUNCH | +24.9% | **LAUNCH** | +29.3% |
| Washington | LAUNCH | +32.0% | PASS | -25.7% |
| Chicago | LAUNCH | +31.3% | PASS | -18.1% |
| Philadelphia | LAUNCH | +23.2% | PASS | -50.1% |
| Atlanta | LAUNCH | +20.0% | PASS | -24.5% |
| Hartford | MONITOR | +37.5% | MONITOR | +2.1% |
| Milwaukee | MONITOR | +33.2% | PASS | -12.0% |
| Cincinnati | MONITOR | +6.0% | PASS | -87.4% |

The README headline, the Streamlit landing metric, the Porter portability
report, the resume material, and the LinkedIn copy all rest on the 5-LAUNCH
number.

### Secondary casualty

`INTEGRITY_REPORT.md:157` showcases Boston as proof that the right-sizing
objective is total contribution rather than margin percentage: "Boston is the
reverse tell: it picks 14x at a *lower* margin percentage than 7x." Under the
corrected arithmetic Boston picks **3x weekly** (LF 0.877, margin +29.3%,
$2.67M contribution) and 14x is deeply negative (-40.6%). No market picks 14x
after the fix, so that demonstration disappears and needs rewriting or
replacing.

### Mitigating context, stated fairly

The author demonstrably understands directionality elsewhere.
`warehouse/reconcile.py:11` reasons explicitly about one-way journeys versus
both-direction onboard counts, and `data/reference/launches_NOTES.md:25`
specifies "initial_weekly_freq is one-way departures per week." This is a
localized slip in one module, not conceptual ignorance. That is exactly why it
needed a unit test and did not have one.

### The fix

1. `models/economics.py`: `deps_yr = freq_wk * 52 * 2`, with a comment naming
   the convention (frequency is round trips; annual flights are both
   directions; demand is bidirectional O&D).
2. Add the regression test that would have caught it (see section 3).
3. Regenerate everything: `make all && make test`.
4. Rewrite the affected reader-facing numbers. `tests/test_consistency.py`
   will fail loudly until you do, which is the suite working as designed.
5. Add a round 4 section to `INTEGRITY_REPORT.md`. The repo's identity is that
   it discloses its own corrections; this is the biggest one and it belongs
   there in the same voice as the others.

### The reframe this forces, and why it is an improvement

"1 LAUNCH out of 204 candidates screened across three studies" is a better
story than 5, not a worse one. It is a screen with teeth, and the one survivor
is the most defensible row in the project: a large anchored market (Boston,
291k pax), entered at modest share, at a conservative 3x weekly, with a real
incumbent set. Lead with that.

## 2. Substantive analytical weaknesses

### 2.1 The flagship study is decided by a single constant

68% of WestJet's candidate set (52 of 76 markets) has demand set to exactly
the unanchored ceiling of 4,000 x corridor growth = 4,659 pax. Median
`demand_pax_yr` across the whole flagship study is 4,659, i.e. the cap.

The cap is well-argued (`LIMITATIONS.md:84`) and is probably the right call.
But it means the flagship's headline finding, "0 LAUNCH", is substantially an
artifact of one hardcoded number rather than an output of the modeling chain.
The honest framing is: *for two thirds of YYC's unserved transborder map, the
2018 survey's silence is the finding, and no model is doing work.* Say that
explicitly, and report how many markets would change verdict if the ceiling
were 8,000 or 12,000.

### 2.2 Declared sensitivities are never executed

`config/assumptions.yaml` attaches a `sensitivity` range to more than twenty
assumptions. Nothing in the codebase reads them. The only scenario analysis
that actually runs is the 3x3 fare/fuel grid. So the README claim that every
assumption carries "a source, a confidence grade, and a sensitivity range" is
true as documentation and false as analysis.

This is the single cheapest high-value addition to the repo. A one-way tornado
over `hurdle_margin_pct`, `airport_fee_proxy`, `transborder_fare_premium`,
`spill_cov`, `min_feasible_load_factor`, `qsi_weights.single_connect`, and the
transfer factor IQR would take an afternoon, would produce the most
interview-ready chart in the project, and would directly answer "how robust is
this verdict?" which is the first question any planning director asks.

Credit where due: the fare grid (-15/0/+10) does effectively span the
transborder premium's own declared range (1.00 to 1.25), so that one
assumption is genuinely stress-tested. Say so.

### 2.3 The backtest does not test the engine

`backtest/run_backtest.py` scores launches by **demand percentile only**. The
thing the project actually produces, the LAUNCH/MONITOR/PASS verdict with its
economics and competition layers, is never scored against outcomes. The report
correctly concludes "demand rank alone does not predict survival" and correctly
says the fix would be economics at launch vintage, but files it as future work.

That is the missing keystone. Running the 48 resolved launches through the full
pre-2022 screen and asking whether the *verdict* separates survivors from
failures is what would elevate this from a well-built pipeline to a validated
one. It is also the most compelling thing you could put in front of an airline.

Two smaller issues in the same module:

- **The transfer factor mathematically cancels.** `candidate_set()` multiplies
  every metro's gravity estimate by the same per-hub `factor`, then takes
  `rank(pct=True)`. A uniform positive scalar cannot change a rank. So the
  docstring's "gravity fit on 2018-2019 US data x transfer factor" overstates
  what the backtest uses; the factor is inert here. Either drop it from the
  backtest or state that it is rank-neutral.
- **Threshold drift.** The backtest's counterfactual candidate set uses
  population >= 250k and distance 250 to 3000 mi, while the live screen uses
  `candidate_min_metro_pop` = 400k and the study config's distance range. The
  backtest is therefore not ranking against the engine's own candidate set.
  Pull both from `assumptions.yaml`.

### 2.4 Revenue is O&D only, with no ancillary and no flow

Two structural omissions on the revenue line, neither currently in LIMITATIONS:

- **No ancillary revenue.** For Porter, and emphatically for the Flair and Lynx
  post-mortems, ancillary is a large share of total revenue. Modeling
  ticket revenue only understates viability for exactly the carrier types the
  project studies.
- **No connecting or beyond revenue.** Every route is scored as pure O&D. A hub
  carrier's transborder route carries flow traffic, which is a material part of
  why WestJet serves what it serves from YYC. This biases the flagship study
  toward PASS in a way that is currently invisible.

Neither needs to be modeled. Both need to be in LIMITATIONS with a direction of
error, which is the standard the rest of the document already meets.

### 2.5 Things I suspected and cleared

Stating these so the review is not one-sided:

- **The linear fare curve is fine.** I fit it against the alternatives on the
  actual 6,715-market DB1B frame. Linear gives R2 = 0.496 with band bias
  between -6.1% and +3.9%; a log-log taper is *worse* (R2 = 0.385). The
  implementation choice is empirically justified. It should still report its
  own fit quality rather than being silent about it.
- **Reproducibility holds.** I ran `make all` from the committed state.
  Verdicts are identical and margins match to 1e-13; the only diff is float64
  last-bit noise from summation order. The determinism claim in
  `INTEGRITY_REPORT.md` is honest.
- **No committed junk.** No `.DS_Store`, no `.duckdb`, no `__pycache__` in git.
  `.gitignore` discipline is good, and the `deploy-prep` force-add of the small
  outputs is a sensible, documented exception.
- **Style rules are actually followed.** Zero em dashes, en dashes, or double
  hyphens across all tracked markdown, Python, and YAML, per CLAUDE.md rule 9.

## 3. Testing: the suite institutionalizes the wrong thing

13 tests over 3,728 lines of Python. The split matters more than the count:

| file | what it actually asserts |
|---|---|
| `test_consistency.py` (6 tests) | string matching: docs contain the same numbers as the artifacts |
| `test_sanity.py` (7 tests) | 3 on spill, 1 haversine, 1 gravity guard, 1 served-market cross-check, 1 transborder-fare guard |

`models/economics.py` (239 lines, the analytical core), `models/demand.py`
(182), `models/competition.py` (196), and `models/screen.py` (163) have **zero
unit tests** between them. Nothing asserts that a given demand, frequency,
gauge, and fare produce a known load factor and margin.

This is why a 2x error shipped through four review passes. The consistency
tests guarantee the docs agree with the artifacts; they cannot tell you the
artifacts are right. Consistency is not correctness, and a suite made mostly of
consistency checks creates a false sense of coverage.

The served-market cross-check against the hand-compiled
`incumbent_network_202604.csv` is the one genuinely excellent test in the repo:
it validates a code path against independently compiled external truth. That is
the model to follow.

**Minimum additions:**

1. A golden-numbers test on `economics`: hand-compute one route end to end
   (demand, share, frequency, seats, fare, cost rates in; LF, margin, BELF,
   contribution out) and assert to a tolerance. This catches the directionality
   bug and every future unit slip.
2. A dimensional-analysis test: assert that a route whose annual bidirectional
   demand exactly fills the annual bidirectional seat supply produces LF = 1.0.
   One line, and it is the exact statement of the bug.
3. A verdict-logic table test on `screen.verdict`: parametrize the corners of
   the LAUNCH/MONITOR/PASS tree including the share guard and the BELF cap.
4. A spill property test: monotone in capacity, converges to mu, never exceeds
   min(mu, C). Partially there; make it property-based.
5. Cross-validate modeled LF against T-100 actuals for the served markets the
   screen excludes. You already have the data and the exclusion list. This is a
   free, powerful validation and would have caught the bug independently.

Also: `models/spill.py:6` references `tests/test_spill.py`, which does not
exist. The spill tests live in `test_sanity.py`.

## 4. Production readiness and engineering

### 4.1 CI proves less than it appears to

`.github/workflows/ci.yml` runs `pytest tests/ -q` only. It never runs the
pipeline. Since the consistency tests compare docs against **committed**
artifacts, a green build proves the docs match whatever artifacts were last
committed, not that the pipeline still produces them. Both could be stale
together and CI would stay green.

Fix: cache the warehouse (or commit a tiny fixture warehouse) and have CI run
`make all` then assert the artifacts are unchanged. That turns CI into a real
regression gate and would make the consistency tests mean what they claim.

### 4.2 Committed artifacts churn on every run

Every `make all` rewrites 14 tracked files with float64 last-bit differences,
so the working tree is dirty after any run. Round derived floats on write (say
6 decimal places for margins and load factors) to eliminate the noise, or stop
tracking the Parquet and publish it as a CI release artifact that Streamlit
pulls. The current state trains you to `git checkout --` reflexively, which is
how real changes get discarded.

### 4.3 Code-level items

- `models/economics.py:167` builds the grid with a five-deep nested Python loop
  appending dicts (markets x gauges x frequencies x 3 fares x 3 fuels). This is
  the slowest part of `make all` and the least readable. Vectorize with a
  cross-join DataFrame; it will also make the golden-numbers test easier to
  write.
- `models/screen.py:122` has a confusing dead-ish expression:
  `m.get("demand_method", m["demand_source"]).fillna(...) if "demand_method" in m else ...`.
  Simplify.
- `models/demand.py:95` does a function-local `import re as _re` and a
  function-local import from `.transfer`. Move to module scope.
- SQL is built with f-string interpolation of `carrier`, `hub`, and year
  throughout (`economics.py:46`, `competition.py:81`). These are
  config-supplied, not user-supplied, so it is not an injection risk in
  practice, but parameterized queries are the habit to have and DuckDB
  supports them.
- No linter or formatter configured. Add `ruff` with a minimal ruleset and run
  it in CI. No type checking; `mypy` on `models/` alone would be cheap.
- `archive/` contains nothing but a `.DS_Store`. Delete the directory.

### 4.4 Dependency and reproducibility hygiene

`requirements.txt` uses open-ended lower bounds (`pandas>=2.1`). For a project
whose entire pitch is reproducibility, pin exact versions in a lockfile and
keep the loose bounds for library consumers. A pandas or statsmodels minor
release can silently change your gravity coefficients.

## 5. Portfolio and positioning

### 5.1 Remove the marketing copy from the repo

`docs/linkedin_posts.md`, `docs/resume_material.md`, `docs/interview_story.md`,
and `docs/demo_script.md` are in the public repository. A reviewer opening
`docs/` sees the project's own promotional apparatus before its methods. It
converts "engineer built a tool" into "candidate built a portfolio piece", and
it primes exactly the adversarial reading that finds the directionality bug.

Move them to a private repo or a gitignored `personal/` directory. Keep
`deploy.md`, `validation.md`, `reconciliation.md`, `backtest.md`,
`LIMITATIONS.md`.

Related: `PROGRESS.md` and `INTEGRITY_REPORT.md` are genuinely good artifacts
and should **stay**. They read as engineering discipline, not marketing. But
`PROGRESS.md:65` still says "6 tests green" when there are 13.

### 5.2 Soften the unverifiable claim

`README.md:151`: "No single open-source project combines DB1B, T-100, Form 41,
gravity, QSI, and route economics end to end." You cannot have verified this,
and a reviewer who knows of one counterexample discounts everything else you
wrote. Rephrase to what you can defend: "I could not find an open-source
project that combines these end to end."

### 5.3 Lead with method and validation, not verdict counts

After the P0 fix the verdict counts are thin (1 LAUNCH across 204 candidates).
That is defensible and even admirable, but it is a weak opening line. The
strongest assets in this project are, in order:

1. The reconciliation to published StatCan totals within 1% at national and
   four airport levels. This is verifiable external validation and almost no
   portfolio project has it.
2. The share model validated against observed DB1B carrier shares at SEA
   (MAE 7.0 points over 503 rows), reported by market structure.
3. The observed-vs-modeled provenance flag on every demand row.
4. LIMITATIONS.md itself.

Open with those. The verdicts are the output; the validation is the credential.

### 5.4 Dashboard gaps

`app/streamlit_app.py` is clean and correctly reads only precomputed outputs.
Two gaps a network planner would notice immediately: there is **no map**, which
is unusual for a route planning tool and is the single most legible visual you
could add; and the "About" tab dumps the raw README rather than orienting a
first-time visitor. A sensitivity slider driving a precomputed tornado would
also land well.

### 5.5 The git history tells on itself

24 commits, 20 of them on 2026-07-20 and 4 on 2026-07-23, with messages like
"S1+S3", "Section 4", "Phase 6 docs". A reviewer reads this as a project
generated in two intensive sessions rather than developed over time. That is
not disqualifying and I would not recommend rewriting history to hide it. But
it does mean the code must withstand scrutiny on its own, because the history
provides no independent evidence of iteration. Going forward, commit the P0 fix
and the new tests as genuine, well-messaged, dated increments.

## 7. What I would say in the hiring debrief

The candidate can do the parts that are hard to teach. He reverse-engineered an
ASP.NET form with rot13-encoded URL parameters to get at TranStats, correctly
kept T-100 segment grain separate from DB1B O&D grain, derived an indirect cost
burden from P-1.2 over P-5.2 rather than assuming one, reconciled his computed

writes clearly, he documents what his model cannot see, and when an earlier

why. That last trait is the one I most want and least often find.

He also shipped a 2x units error in the one calculation the entire product
depends on, and built a test suite that could not have caught it because it
tests whether the documentation agrees with the output rather than whether the
output is right.

Both of those are true at once. The first set is character and cannot be
coached in a quarter. The second is a habit and can be, and the fix is already
specified above. My recommendation is hire, conditional on him finding this
class of error himself when probed, and the fastest way for him to make that
probe a non-event is to find it, fix it, and write it up before anyone else
reads the repo.
